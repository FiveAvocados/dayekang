What inspired this project

The film explores the life and imagination of Ishaan, an 8-year-old dyslexic child. Although he excels in art, his poor academic performance leads his parents to send him to a boarding school. Ishaan's new art teacher suspects that he is dyslexic and helps him to overcome his disability. (From Wikipedia)

I became aware of dyslexia after watching the movie ‘Like Stars on Earth’ (2007), which is about a dyslexic child and an art teacher who understands his difficulty and helps him learn to read. After researching how dyslexia is treated in Korea, I found that there was almost no research on or understanding of dyslexia. There was also no digital service specifically designed to help dyslexic children practice Korean at home. About 330,000 children in Korea have dyslexia. Even though they have potential, they are neglected by the current education system, which focuses on reading. Through my work, I wanted to help dyslexic children and increase awareness of dyslexia.

Background

Around 5 to 10 percent of children worldwide have dyslexia.

Of the 50 million people in Korea, 5 million have dyslexia.

The Korean language has its own writing and pronunciation systems.

Problems

Missed Opportunity

There is a crucial timeframe for treating dyslexia. Once this window is missed, the symptoms get worse.

Low Self-Esteem

Dyslexia can cause low self-esteem and other mental health problems in a school environment that focuses on reading. Dyslexic children are often mocked by their peers.

Expensive Treatment

Treatment is too expensive, and most people waste money on unproven methods.

No Guidance for Parents

Parents have a hard time teaching and providing guidance to their dyslexic children.

In Korea, awareness and understanding of dyslexia are limited compared with other countries. It is essential to treat dyslexia before children reach the age of 8, yet many children go through life undiagnosed. Parents also have a hard time guiding dyslexic children, and there are few materials that explain dyslexia treatment to them.

About Dyslexia

What Is Dyslexia?

Dyslexia is a reading disorder characterized by difficulty reading accurately and fluently, as well as difficulty with spelling. Reading is a big part of the Korean curriculum. If children fail to overcome dyslexia at an early age, they can be bullied at school, leading to low self-esteem. The trauma from childhood can affect them well into adulthood.

Symptoms

Symptoms are often first noticed by a teacher or parent when children are around 8 or 9 years old. Teachers and parents can recognize the signs when children's academic performance falls behind that of their peers. By ages 9 or 10, children may have difficulty reading one-syllable words or understanding phonology. They have difficulty understanding the order of consonants and vowels. By age 12, they often make spelling mistakes and show poor writing ability. They also have difficulty memorizing dates, people's names, and numbers. Even in adulthood, they may still have difficulty reading and continue to make spelling mistakes.

Is Dyslexia Curable?

Yes. In the past, people thought dyslexia was caused by visual impairments. However, research in cognitive psychology has shown that dyslexia is a neurological disorder in the left hemisphere of the brain. The left hemisphere of a person with dyslexia cannot store text and sounds or combine them quickly. If dyslexia is diagnosed after age 10, it is much harder to treat. Younger brains are more flexible and more likely to form neural circuits properly. Therefore, early detection is critical. Once children miss this timeframe, they must make greater efforts to keep up with their peers.

How to Treat Dyslexia: What Are the Problems with Current Methods?

Phonology should be taught by linking written forms to sounds. However, there are problems with current education methods.

  1. Books currently used in medical institutions and homes to treat dyslexic children are often difficult to understand. As a result, children lose interest in learning Hangeul and often refuse to take classes.
  2. It is difficult to keep up with expensive private classes that cost more than $100 per hour.
  3. There is a lack of medical centers that specialize in treating dyslexia. These centers are often far from where dyslexic children live. Sometimes, parents fly from Jeju Island to visit a medical center in Seoul.

We wrote down keywords from the interviews and then rearranged them into categories.

Persona 1: Min-Jung Kim / 38 years old

Situation: She is a working mom who raises a dyslexic child. Thinking: She wants to spark her child's interest in reading and practice Korean with him every day.

Conditions: Min-Jung Kim is an office worker who has an eight-year-old son. He has difficulty reading or writing Korean compared with other children his age. She suspected that he had dyslexia and enrolled him in a special class at an institution once a week. As time went by, she felt burdened by the high tuition and the distance to the medical center. Her son was also not very interested in learning Korean. Eventually, she gave up on the treatment, and now she plans to buy dyslexia education books and teach Korean to her son. However, her son tries to run away. To teach him in person, Min-Jung also has to study Korean phonics so she can teach her son effectively. However, she feels frustrated because she does not have enough time to spare.

Needs: 1) I want to diagnose my child at home if he is suspected of having dyslexia. 2) I want to treat dyslexia anywhere and at a low cost. 3) I hope there is an engaging educational service that can interest my child. 4) I want to teach Korean as frequently as possible. 5) I want to see my child's progress.

Persona 2: Jee-Hee Park / 8 years old

Situation: He has low self-esteem because of his poor reading skills. Thinking: He wants to read as well as his friends and wants others to understand his difficulties.

Conditions: He feels left behind because his peers are good at reading, but he isn't. He wants to avoid reading in front of the class. He is often seen as problematic and lazy, even though poor reading is only a symptom of dyslexia that he cannot control. He gets angry because his parents treat him like a problem child because he cannot read. This misunderstanding causes trouble within the family. In classes with a language therapist who specializes in dyslexia, he enjoys learning Korean, even when it is difficult, because the therapist guides him well. However, when he is learning Korean at home with his parents, he becomes less interested because his parents are not as good as the therapist at guiding him. This worsens the relationship between the child and his parents. Instead of learning Korean, he wants to play games all day. Because he hates reading, his confidence has decreased and his grades have dropped.

Needs: 1) I want learning Korean to feel like a fun game. 2) I want to read as well as my friends. 3) I don't want to fight with my parents. 4) I want others to understand my difficulties.

Experience Mapping: Parents' Point of View

Discover: Parents start to recognize that their children are falling behind their peers. If parents do not observe them carefully at this stage, they may not take the symptoms seriously and may miss the treatment window. Parents may confuse dyslexia with other conditions, such as ADHD or other learning difficulties. There is a risk of pursuing non-scientific practices such as oriental medicine or eye training to improve reading ability.

Treatment: A specialist confirms that the patient has dyslexia. The child starts a dyslexia therapy program, visits once or twice a week, and works with language therapists. Therapists often give homework to encourage steady learning; however, the homework is often left undone. The symptoms may begin to improve.

Giving Up: Parents try to teach Korean to the child at home. The child refuses to learn because the parents are not teachers. Since parents are not experts, it is hard to teach children without proper guidelines.

Solution Points: If users suspect dyslexia, they can use the Diagnostic function in the app to receive guidance on whether to visit a specialized institution for a diagnosis. With the application, children can continue learning Korean at their own pace when they are not attending classes at the institution. Exciting content in the app encourages children to study with their parents without a teacher. The application also provides parents with guidance on how to support their children. If the parents are too busy to teach, children can study by themselves.

Design Insights

“An engaging service that helps dyslexic children study Korean at home and practice consistently”

Service Overview

Who: Dyslexic children from 5 to 10 years old
What: Korean phonics practice using nonwords
When: Every day at home when they do not visit the specialist institution
How: Actively and continuously, by themselves or with parents

Concept

We combined content from the book ‘Reading Confidence’ with the story of ‘Alice in Wonderland’ to provide more engaging learning experiences. We saw similarities between dyslexic children experiencing strange letters and Alice meeting unusual characters in Wonderland. We therefore applied the story of Alice in Wonderland to our app design. We transformed the books into digital multimedia content. Children can use multimedia content on their digital devices to practice Korean. After imitating the mouth shape, children can record themselves and compare their movements with the correct answer.

Design Ideas

To maintain the benefits of professional dyslexia treatment and maximize the benefits of online and digital content, we embraced the following methods: Multisensory Instruction, Storytelling, Personalized Instruction, and Gamification.

1. Multisensory Instruction

We decided to use multisensory instruction to enable children to learn Hangeul by using various senses such as sound and touch.

2. Storytelling

Alice meets the characters who represent each chapter and travels through each stage to return to reality. The structure follows a ‘growth epic’. We created a fantasy setting for the game to stimulate children's imaginations. Fun and friendly characters help children learn Hangeul and overcome emotional trauma.

→

3. Personalized Instruction

The application provides guidance through learning steps based on each child's needs. Children can focus on improving their weaker areas by checking them on the ‘report’.

4. Gamification

On ‘My page’, children can see their progress. Gamification of the learning process can help children become interested in learning Korean. The application gives positive feedback and encouragement while children play the game. It encourages them to continue learning Korean voluntarily. The application uses a reward system. Children earn badges whenever they make progress.

Low-fi Prototype

Diagnosis

The app diagnoses dyslexia by assessing how well children understand the principles of consonants and vowels. Based on the results, it assesses their level of fluency. The app plays instructions that guide children to replace consonants or vowels based on what they heard (for example, which vowel they need to change ‘Nack’ to ‘Nock’). The app asks children to record their voices while reading the text in the box within a set amount of time. It measures how fluently they read the text.

Learning Phonics

Using multimedia content, children learn how to pronounce letters. The app shows mouth movements and how the tongue is positioned within the mouth to teach children how to pronounce each consonant and vowel.

Phonics Game

Based on the phonics that children have learned, they play a phonics game. Children listen to the pronunciation of vowels and then select the answers in order.

Understanding Phonemes

Children learn how phonemes are pronounced when they are next to each other. When children combine specific phoneme blocks, the app shows how adjacent phonemes are pronounced.

Report

They receive a report based on their play session. The child and their parents can view the child's progress in visual graphs, and the report provides guidance to the parents. The report page shows children and parents how much they are improving, identifies common mistakes, and provides feedback.

Test & Development

Wizard of Oz Testing with a Dyslexic Child and Her Caregiver

We tested the prototype with an 8-year-old dyslexic child, ‘Ji-Yoon’. First, we asked the father to teach the child how to pronounce certain vowels using the textbook used in offline lessons. Then, we asked Ji-Yoon to learn other vowels using the application. We received qualitative feedback from both the father and Ji-Yoon indicating that the application made learning Korean phonics more fun. The father mentioned that it reduced the burden of teaching phonics to the child and that the application served as a guide for him.

Left: learning pronunciation with the parent using a book · Right: Ji-Yoon is using the application instead of the book to learn Korean vowels on her own

Expert Interview

“Instead of regular words, dyslexic children should practice non-words”
Dr. Jae-Seok Jung, dyslexia expert and author of ‘Reading Confidence’

Even though we received positive feedback from the dyslexic child and the caregiver, we wanted feedback from an expert. We interviewed Dr. Jae-Seok Jung, a dyslexia expert who wrote ‘Reading Confidence’ for dyslexic children. It is the only book in Korea designed especially for dyslexic children. Because our application is based on the book's content, we thought it was natural to ask for his opinion on the application design. He emphasized the importance of practicing ‘non-words’ because dyslexic children tend to memorize whole words instead of understanding phonics. Therefore, it is essential for children to practice phonics with non-words. This practice helps them read new words naturally when they encounter them.

What are non-words? A non-word is a combination of letters that has no meaning. Regular words: Cat, Museum. Non-words: Caet, Mooseum.

  • “We need to teach them in the form of individual characters, unlike the usual Hangeul education apps that are currently available on the market.”
  • “We definitely need games for dyslexic children to help them enjoy learning Korean at home.”
  • “Instead of trying to put all the content from the book into the app, I believe it is better to focus on repetitive training.”

Developing the Prototype for the Non-words Block Game

Based on the expert interview, we decided to develop the app further by creating a multi-sensory game that enables children to use word blocks to combine words. This idea came from previous studies suggesting that block play improves reading skills.¹ After hearing non-words pronounced, the user creates them using letter blocks. The webcam then records the activity and sends data to the computer. We used the software ‘Processing’ and image recognition to provide feedback on whether the answer is correct.

1. Effect of Block Play on Language Acquisition and Attention in Toddlers. Dimitri A. Christakis, MD, MPH; Frederick J. Zimmerman, PhD; Michelle M. Garrison, PhD. American Medical Association
