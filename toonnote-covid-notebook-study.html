Coronaviruses are a group of related RNA viruses that cause diseases in mammals and birds. In humans and birds, they cause respiratory tract infections that can range from mild to lethal. Mild illnesses in humans include some cases of the common cold (which is also caused by other viruses, predominantly rhinoviruses), while more lethal varieties can cause SARS, MERS, and COVID-19. In cows and pigs they cause diarrhea, while in mice they cause hepatitis and encephalomyelitis. There are as yet no vaccines or antiviral drugs to prevent or treat human coronavirus infections.

Interpretation of the epidemic curve

The cumulative curve remains comparatively flat through February, begins to accelerate in late March, and rises steeply through the summer. By August 12, 2020 the dataset records more than 20.6 million confirmed cases worldwide. The increasing slope is important: it indicates that large numbers of new infections were still being added each day, even after the first wave had moved beyond China and Europe.

Recovered cases also rise over time, but they lag behind confirmed infections. Active cases therefore remain substantial throughout the period, while deaths accumulate more slowly but consistently.

How the geographic pattern changed

The maps show a clear change in the center of the outbreak. China dominates the earliest observations. By the end of March, Italy is also prominent, while by April the United States has become a major hotspot. The June views show that high confirmed totals are no longer concentrated in one region: the United States, Brazil, and India represent successive large outbreaks across North America, South America, and South Asia.

The state-level view adds another layer of detail. California, Florida, and Texas have the highest cumulative totals in the August 12 snapshot, demonstrating that a national total can conceal substantial regional concentration.

Analytical limitations and conclusion

  • Confirmed counts depend on testing access, case definitions, and reporting practices, all of which differ by place and date.
  • Deaths and recoveries occur after confirmation, so direct same-day comparisons do not represent outcome probabilities.
  • Active cases are derived as confirmed minus deaths minus recovered and inherit uncertainty from every source field.
  • The choropleth maps show absolute totals rather than population-adjusted rates, so larger countries and states should not be interpreted as having greater individual risk solely from color intensity.

Overall, this exploratory analysis describes a rapid transition from a localized outbreak to a distributed global pandemic. The combined temporal and geographic views make the change visible, while the limitations above are essential for interpreting the patterns as descriptive evidence rather than causal explanation.
